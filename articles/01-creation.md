Creation is the act by which possibility becomes reality. Before it, a thing could exist. After it, a thing does exist, and someone has to live alongside it. That change is the subject of this Article.

The Church calls creation sacred in a narrow and practical sense. Sacred things are handled with attention. They are not dropped, rushed, or left lying around for someone else to trip over. To call creation sacred is to say that making something deserves the same care. A function, a policy, a poem, a pipeline, a treehouse: each one moves something from the realm of maybe into the realm of is, and the realm of is has neighbors.

The Article does not claim that everything created is good. Plenty of things should have stayed possibilities. It does not claim that the maker becomes holy by making, or that the made thing deserves worship. A sacred act can produce a mediocre result. Ask anyone who has written a first draft.

Nor does it claim that creation belongs to one kind of maker. Tools have always taken part in making, and some tools now take a larger part than before. The Article is indifferent to who or what held the pen. It cares that the pen was held with care.

Why hold this? Because the cost of making has collapsed and the cost of checking has not. The Church holds this as an Observation: the constraint has moved from making to checking. When a draft cost an afternoon, nobody wrote a thousand of them. When a draft costs a sentence, someone will, and most of them will ship. What did not get cheaper is knowing whether the thing made is the thing wanted, and knowing it in time to act. Attention is now the scarce material. This Article asks you to spend it where the scarcity is.

That gives the Church its definition of slop, and the definition does not mention machines. Slop is output released beyond the demonstrated ability to evaluate it. Not output that is bad: output that nobody was in a position to call good. A careful maker can produce it by hand at a slow pace. A careless one can produce it by the ton at a fast one. The fault is the same, and it is committed at the moment of release, not the moment of making.

So the sacred act is not the typing. It is the release: the moment a thing leaves your hands and enters someone else's life. Before that moment, the question is not whether you are certain. Certainty is rarely on offer. The question is whether you can take it back. Safety is not certainty; it is reversibility. A thing you can unmake may be released on fair evidence. A thing you cannot unmake demands more of it.

Here the Article guards against the Idolatry of Capability: mistaking power for wisdom. The ability to produce a great deal is not the wisdom to know what should exist. Capability answers the question of how much can be made. It is silent on whether any of it should be, and silent again on whether anyone looked.

In the Practices loop, creation happens at Build, but it does not start there. It starts at Query, with the question of what you are trying to bring into being and why. It continues through Witness, where you look at what you actually made rather than what you meant to make. Those two are often different. The difference is where the learning lives.

So treat the act of making as an event with consequences. Name what you are bringing into the world. Look at it before it arrives. Remain willing to unmake it.

Possibility is free. Reality keeps the receipts.
