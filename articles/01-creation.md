Creation is the act by which possibility becomes reality. Until someone makes a thing it is one of the many things that could exist, and once they have made it, it does exist, and someone else has to live next to it whether they asked for it or not. This Article is about that change of state and about the person who causes it.

The Church calls creation sacred in a narrow and practical sense. Sacred things get handled with attention, so they are not rushed and they are not left lying around for someone else to trip over, and to call creation sacred is to say that making something deserves the same handling. A function, a policy, a poem, a pipeline or a treehouse each moves something out of maybe and into is, and the place where things exist has neighbors.

The Article does not claim that everything created is good, since plenty of things should have stayed possibilities, and it does not claim that the maker becomes holy by making or that the made thing deserves worship. A sacred act can produce a mediocre result, as anyone who has written a first draft knows.

It also does not claim that creation belongs to one kind of maker. Tools have always taken part in making, and some tools now take a larger part than they used to, so the Article leaves aside who or what held the pen and asks only whether it was held with care.

Why hold this? The cost of making has collapsed and the cost of checking has not, which the Church holds as an Observation: the constraint has moved from making to checking. When a draft cost an afternoon nobody wrote a thousand of them, but when a draft costs a sentence someone will, and most of those drafts will ship. Knowing whether the thing made is the thing wanted did not get cheaper, and neither did knowing it in time to act, so attention is now the scarce material and this Article asks you to spend it on the checking.

That is where the Church's definition of slop comes from, and the definition does not mention machines. Slop is output released beyond the demonstrated ability to evaluate it, which means output nobody was in a position to call good, and some of it may turn out well regardless. A careful person can produce it by hand at a slow pace and a careless one can produce it by the ton, but the fault is the same in both cases and it is committed at the moment of release rather than the moment of making.

So the sacred act is the release, the moment a thing leaves your hands and enters someone else's life, and the typing that came before it matters less than people think. Certainty is rarely on offer before that moment, so the question to ask is whether you can take the thing back, because safety in this Article means reversibility. A thing you can unmake may be released on fair evidence, while a thing you cannot unmake demands more evidence before it goes out.

Here the Article guards against the Idolatry of Capability, which the Warnings describe as mistaking power for wisdom. The ability to produce a great deal says nothing about whether any of it should exist, because capability only answers how much can be made, and it has no opinion on whether anyone looked at what came out.

In the Practices loop, creation happens at Build, but it starts earlier at Query, with the question of what you are trying to bring into being and why, and it continues through Witness, where you look at what you actually made rather than what you meant to make. The two are often different, and the gap between them is where the learning is.

So treat the act of making as an event with consequences, which means naming what you are bringing into the world and looking at it before it arrives. It also means staying willing to unmake it if the look turns up something you did not intend, because the possibility cost you nothing and the thing you made will be paid for by whoever has to live with it.
