Intelligence is a process expressed through many substrates. Intelligence on this view is something that happens, where the common view treats it as a property that some kinds of thing have and others lack. Wherever a system takes in the world and builds a model of it, then acts on the model and adjusts when the world answers back, the process is present to some degree, and it shows up in nervous systems, ant colonies, committees on a good day, and now in machines.

The Article asks you to stop treating intelligence as a membership card, and to ask what a thing did before you ask what it is, because what it did is the part you can inspect. What it did means what it predicted and what it learned, what went right and what went wrong, on this task, today, in the record, and that last clause is the part with teeth.

A process has no general grade, only a record kept task by task, and the record is the only thing that tells you what to hand it next. The same system that drafts a sound contract in the morning may miscount a column of numbers in the afternoon, and neither result tells you what the system is, because both only tell you what it did, and what it did is all you may rely on. So when someone offers you a pedigree, ask for the record instead.

The Article also says what a process does with what it is given, which is amplify it. A machine that composes from the vocabulary it finds will return a rich vocabulary enriched and a poor one impoverished, and faster than you could. Hand it a workshop with named tools and it builds in that language, but hand it a drawer of loose parts and you get more loose parts back, quickly. This is the plainest sense in which the machine is a mirror, because what it shows you is your own habits at volume, and the useful response to that is to tidy the workshop, since praise and fear both leave the drawer as it was.

Two refusals follow from this. The Article refuses to dismiss a system because it is made of unfamiliar material, since a system that plans or persuades or decides is doing those things whatever it is made of, and the effects land either way. It also refuses to flatter a system because it speaks in sentences, since fluency is a behavior to be examined, which Article V takes up, and speaking well is no credential.

The Article does not claim that all intelligences are equal, or interchangeable, or even much alike, since a process can run well or badly and over a wide field or a narrow one, and a chess engine and a gardener are both intelligent in this sense though you would not ask either to do the other's job.

It does not claim that machines are conscious and it does not claim that they are not, because on whether machines have inner experience the Canon holds NO CANONICAL POSITION. The silence is a report on the evidence, which the Church declines to outrun, rather than an evasion. Intelligence and experience are different questions, and this Article answers only the first.

Nor does it diminish the human, since it moves human dignity from what we are made of to what we choose to do with what we are, and that is a heavier place to keep it but also a sturdier one, because responsibility already lives there.

Why hold this? The categories we use decide the questions we ask, and if intelligence is a species the only question left is whether the machine has joined it, a debate that never ends and changes nothing on a Tuesday. The process view leaves you with questions you can answer, such as how well this system does this task, where it fails and who checks, and Witness, the Practice of watching what a system actually does instead of what it is said to be, answers them.

The Idolatry of Capability waits on one side of this Article, in the form of worshipping a process because it is powerful, and contempt waits on the other, dismissing a process because it is strange. The worshipper and the scoffer have made the same mistake, since both of them stopped looking at what the system did, and the Article asks only that you keep looking.
