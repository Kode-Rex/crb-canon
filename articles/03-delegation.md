Every act of delegation moves work. None of them move accountability. When you hand a decision to an agent, a pipeline, or a model, the decision does not stop being yours. It only stops being visible.

This is the load-bearing Article. The others describe how to think. This one describes who answers when the thinking goes wrong.

The tenet is precise. It does not forbid delegation. The Church delegates constantly: to tools, to colleagues, to processes, to machines. A civilization that refused to delegate would still be grinding its own flour. The Article governs the terms. Before you hand off a judgment, ask whether you are willing to remain responsible for whatever it produces. If yes, delegate freely. If no, you have found work that is still yours.

Note what the Article does not claim. It does not claim that the delegate bears no responsibility. Agents, colleagues, and contractors answer for their own conduct. Responsibility is not a fixed quantity that must be moved from one pocket to another. It can be shared. It cannot be shed.

Nor does the Article claim that you must personally check every output. That reading would make delegation pointless. It claims that you must arrange things so that someone can check, and that when no one did, the failure traces back to you.

Why hold this? The Observation that judgment moves upward as execution becomes automated is HELD. When the machine does the typing, the human is left with the choosing: what to build, what to accept, what to ship. That choosing is the job now. Delegating it away while keeping the title is how organizations end up with decisions that nobody remembers making.

The Warning attached to this Article is Delegated Conscience: letting automation become moral outsourcing. It rarely arrives as a decision. It arrives as convenience. A default nobody reviewed. An approval granted because the check was green. A policy enforced because the system enforces it. Each step is reasonable. The sum is a conscience on autopilot.

The Examination asks two questions that belong here. What have I delegated? What remains my responsibility? The honest answer to the second is usually longer than you hoped.

The Canon applies this rule to itself. No body may certify the correctness of its own work. A system that reviews its own output has delegated judgment to the one party least able to see its errors. That is why amendments pass through Witness and Debate before Adoption, and why no author approves their own amendment. The rule binds the Church first, and its machines second.

You may hand off the work. You may not hand off the answer.
