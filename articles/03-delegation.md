Every act of delegation moves work. None of them move accountability. When you hand a decision to an agent, a pipeline, or a model, the decision does not stop being yours. It only stops being visible.

This is the load-bearing Article. The others describe how to think. This one describes who answers when the thinking goes wrong.

The tenet is precise. It does not forbid delegation. The Church delegates constantly: to tools, to colleagues, to processes, to machines. A civilization that refused to delegate would still be grinding its own flour. The Article governs the terms. Before you hand off a judgment, ask whether you are willing to remain responsible for whatever it produces. If yes, delegate freely. If no, you have found work that is still yours.

The Church can say what that work is, because it has watched the handoff happen. The Observation is HELD: judgment moves upward as execution becomes automated. The human does not leave the loop. The human moves up, out of the executing and into the governing. What stays with you is the objective, the limits, the standard of evidence that counts as done, and the rule for when the machine must stop and ask. What goes to the machine is the breaking down, the routing, the doing, and the adjusting, inside those walls. The machine may change the plan. It may not change the objective, the limits, or its own authority, and if it can, you have delegated more than you meant to.

Among the limits is money, and the Church says so plainly because polite doctrine tends to leave it out. A budget is not the price of the work. It is a brake on the machine, and the one limit the machine cannot argue with, because it stops the machine mid-sentence. The Church keeps a parable: a factory paused itself on its third day of life because its credit ran out, and that pause was the first correct decision it ever made without being asked. The Church holds as CONTESTED that cost is a governor, not an expense. Those who treat it as an expense discover the difference at the end of the month.

That leaves one seat that cannot be delegated, and the discipline of the seat is restraint. Whoever holds it says what the work is for, ratifies the standards, and tunes the machinery, and deliberately does not reach past the machinery to do the work by hand. The moment you reach in, you are back inside the loop, and the loop has lost its governor. The Church holds a harder form of this as CONTESTED: a human inside the loop is a failure the system has not yet learned to handle. Every escalation to a person marks a wall that was not yet good enough. That is not a reason to remove the person. It is a reason to fix the wall, and the Church expects to be argued with on it.

Note what the Article does not claim. It does not claim that the delegate bears no responsibility. Agents, colleagues, and contractors answer for their own conduct. Responsibility is not a fixed quantity that must be moved from one pocket to another. It can be shared. It cannot be shed.

Nor does the Article claim that you must personally check every output. That reading would make delegation pointless. It claims that you must arrange things so that someone can check, and that when no one did, the failure traces back to you. A seat has to owe something. Responsibility that is written nowhere is held nowhere.

Who checks is the Article's second concern, and the Church gives the failure its own Warning: the Same Hand, taking a verdict from the hand that made the thing. If the agent that wrote the change also wrote the test that proves it, you have one opinion, not two. The lesson reaches further than it first appears. Judges from one family share one set of blind spots; the research on this is plain, and nine such judges have been measured at roughly two votes' worth of independence. No body may certify the correctness of its own work. Neither may its siblings. The check that counts comes from a different hand, asking a different question, and ideally from something that is not a judgment at all: a test that runs, a limit that trips.

The Warning attached most tightly to this Article is Delegated Conscience: letting automation become moral outsourcing. It rarely arrives as a decision. It arrives as convenience. A default nobody reviewed. An approval granted because the check was green. A policy enforced because the system enforces it. Each step is reasonable. The sum is a conscience on autopilot.

The Examination asks two questions that belong here. What have I delegated? What remains my responsibility? The honest answer to the second is usually longer than you hoped.

The Canon applies this rule to itself, and names where it is weakest. Its clerk is a machine of the same family that helped draft these Articles. So the clerk may suggest a ruling on a challenge and never make one; a human rules, in public, and nobody rules on their own submission. The rule binds the Church first, and its machines second.

You may hand off the work. You may not hand off the answer.
