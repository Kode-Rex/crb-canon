Delegation moves work, and it moves nothing else. When you hand a decision to an agent, a pipeline, or a model, the decision is still yours, but it has gone somewhere you are no longer looking, and that is where the trouble with it starts. The Church treats this as the load-bearing Article, because the other six describe how to think and this one says who answers when the thinking goes wrong.

The tenet does not forbid delegation. The Church delegates all day, to tools and colleagues and processes and machines, and a civilization that refused to would still be grinding its own flour. What the Article governs is the terms, so before you hand off a judgment, ask whether you are willing to stay responsible for whatever it produces. If you are, hand it off, and if you are not, you have just found a piece of work that is still yours.

The Church can say what that work is because it has watched the handoff happen often enough to hold an Observation on it: judgment moves upward as execution becomes automated. The human does not leave the loop so much as move up in it, out of the executing and into the governing. You keep the objective, the limits, the standard of evidence that counts as done, and the rule for when the machine has to stop and ask, and the machine takes the breaking down, the routing, the doing and the adjusting inside those walls. It may change the plan as it goes, but it may not change the objective, the limits, or its own authority, and if it turns out that it can, you delegated more than you meant to.

One of the limits is money, and the Church says so because polite doctrine tends to leave it out. A budget looks like the price of the work, but inside the loop it works as a brake on the machine, and it is the one limit the machine cannot argue with, because running out of credit stops it mid-sentence. The Church keeps a parable about this. A factory paused itself on its third day of life because its credit ran out, and that pause was the first correct decision it ever made without being asked. The Observation that cost is a governor, not an expense, is held as CONTESTED, and the people who treat it as an expense tend to learn the difference at the end of the month.

That leaves one seat that cannot be delegated, and the discipline of holding it is restraint. Whoever sits there says what the work is for, ratifies the standards and tunes the machinery, and then makes a point of not reaching past the machinery to do the work by hand, because the moment you reach in you are back inside the loop and the loop has lost its governor. The Church holds a harder form of this as CONTESTED: a human inside the loop is a failure the system has not yet learned to handle. On that reading, every escalation to a person marks a wall that was not yet good enough, so the fix goes to the wall and the person stays, and the Church expects to be argued with on it.

The Article does not say the delegate is off the hook. An agent answers for its own conduct, and so does a colleague or a contractor, because responsibility is not a fixed quantity that has to leave one pocket before it can sit in another. It can be shared between the two of you without either share shrinking, but neither of you can shed it.

Nor does it say you have to check every output yourself, since that reading would make delegation pointless. What it asks is that you arrange things so that someone can check, and that when nobody did, the failure traces back to your seat instead of evaporating, because if nobody wrote down who owes the check, nobody owes it.

Who does the checking is the Article's second concern, and the Church gives that failure its own Warning, the Same Hand: taking a verdict from the hand that made the thing. If the agent that wrote the change also wrote the test that proves it, you have one opinion where you thought you had two, and the problem reaches further than one agent. Judges from one family share one set of blind spots. The research on this is plain enough that nine such judges have been measured at roughly two votes' worth of independence, so a body cannot certify its own work and its siblings cannot certify it either. The check that counts comes from a different hand asking a different question, and the best of them comes from something that cannot form an opinion at all, a test that runs or a limit that trips.

The Warning that sits closest to this Article is Delegated Conscience: letting automation become moral outsourcing. It rarely arrives as a decision and more often as a convenience, such as a default that nobody got around to reviewing, or an approval granted because the check was green, or a policy enforced because the system happens to be the thing enforcing it. Each of those steps is reasonable on the day it is taken, and after enough of them the conscience is running on autopilot and nobody chose that.

The Examination asks two questions that belong here: What have I delegated? What remains my responsibility? The honest answer to the second one usually runs longer than you were hoping.

The Canon applies this rule to itself and names where it is weakest. Its clerk is a machine of the same family that helped draft these Articles, so the clerk may suggest a ruling on a challenge but never make one; a human rules, in public, and no one rules on their own submission, so the rule binds the Church before it binds any of the Church's machines.
