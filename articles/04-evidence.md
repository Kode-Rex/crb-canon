Belief must remain vulnerable to observation. That is the whole tenet, and the word doing the work in it is vulnerable, because a belief that nothing could injure is sealed rather than strong, and a sealed belief keeps well for years without teaching its holder anything.

The Article does not ask you to stop believing, which would be impossible and, frankly, rude. Everyone acts on beliefs they have never checked for themselves, since nobody could get through a morning otherwise: that the bridge will hold, that the colleague meant well, that the test suite is testing something. The Article asks only that a belief keep a door open, so that when the world says otherwise the world can get in.

It also ranks the two, so when belief and evidence disagree, evidence wins and the belief is the thing revised. The pull runs the other way most days, because it is easier to reread the data until it agrees with us, or to call the failing case an edge case, or to develop sudden doubts about the methodology behind a result we dislike.

The Article does not claim that evidence is infallible. Someone gathered it and framed it, and someone read it, so it can be partial or stale or wrong, and the remedy for bad evidence is better evidence rather than firmer belief. You are free to challenge an observation, but you are not free to ignore it.

Nor is the Article a judgment on religious faith. It governs claims that observation can reach, such as what a system does, whether a change worked, and what happened in production on Thursday, and it leaves the interior life of believers alone because it has no business there.

The Church holds this Article because claims have become cheap and confident, and the cheapest of them are claims of success, so it keeps an Observation on the point: the claim of a fix is not the fix. Only the record is. A machine will tell you it fixed the thing, in a comment and in good grammar, and the comment and the fix are two different events. The Church keeps two parables about this. In the first, a factory carried a dozen branches, each one announcing a repair, and every announcement was sincere, but the trunk, which is the only record that counts, had received none of them. In the second, a fifth of a test suite went uncollected for months because of a naming accident, and every build in that time was green, because green meant the tests that ran had passed and said nothing about the tests that never ran. From far enough away, silence looks like success, and most of us read our builds from far away.

Two rules follow from that. The first is that an instruction is a hypothesis, because every sentence you give a machine is a claim about what it will do when it reads it, and the record is the experiment that tests the claim. The second is that a check nobody has ever seen fail has not been shown to check anything, so break the thing on purpose and watch for the alarm, and if the alarm stays quiet you have been keeping an ornament where you thought you had a guard.

The Warning here is the Comfortable Oracle: accepting answers because further questions are inconvenient. The Oracle does not have to lie to you, because all it needs is for you to stop checking.

The Church tries to practice what this Article preaches. Each of its Observations carries a status, HELD or CONTESTED for most of them, and each of those can move, because the Canon is versioned so that reality may amend it, and an amendment cannot reach Adoption without first passing through Evidence. A doctrine that refused revision by evidence would fail its own fourth Article.

In the Practices loop this Article lives at Witness and Reflect, where you look at what happened and then let it change you, and in the Examination it supplies the hardest of the four questions: What evidence would prove me wrong? If you cannot name any, the belief is unfalsifiable rather than strong, and that is a different thing and a worse one.
