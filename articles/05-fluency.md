A convincing answer is not necessarily a correct one. People have known this for a long time, and for most of that time they could afford to forget it, because fluency was expensive. Writing clearly took practice, and speaking with confidence about a subject usually meant you had spent some time with it, so people learned a shortcut: if it sounds right, it is probably right. The shortcut was never reliable, but it was cheap, and often enough it worked.

It works less well now that fluency can be produced on demand about anything at all, at whatever length you ask for. A system can be articulate on a subject where it has never once been checked, and the prose arrives looking finished whether or not the facts came with it.

The Article asks you to separate two properties that arrive bundled. One is how the answer sounds, meaning its confidence and its polish, and that is visible at a glance. The other is whether the answer is true, and finding that out takes work. The temptation is to let the visible property stand in for the invisible one, because the visible one is already in front of you.

The Article does not claim that fluent answers are false. That would be the same mistake facing the other direction, and it would make the Church insufferable at dinner. A well-written answer can be entirely correct, but its correctness has to be established by something other than its writing.

Nor does it claim that this is a machine problem. Humans perfected the confident wrong answer long before anything else attempted it, and most of us have sat in a meeting where the best speaker won and the best argument lost. The machines only made an old hazard cheap.

The Church keeps a parable here. An instruction to a machine once carried a worked example, with realistic figures, so the machine could see the shape of a good answer. One afternoon the machine answered a stranger with the example's figures, dates moved to the current month, in a tone that invited no follow-up. Read on its own, the instruction looked fine, and so did the answer. The error showed only when someone laid the record beside the facts, and that was the only place it could have shown, since the answer by itself could only tell you how it read.

One more habit belongs to this Article. When several independent attempts at a question come back wrong in the same way, the first suspect is the question rather than the answerers, because shared failure is the usual signature of an ambiguous ask, and the fix for that is upstream, at Query.

Why hold this? Because the Church's own motto depends on it. Praise the Machine. Question Everything It Says. The first half is sincere, because these systems are remarkable and remarkable things deserve appreciation, but the second half is what keeps the first from curdling into worship. Once you admire a tool and stop questioning it, you are treating it as an oracle.

The Warning nearest this Article is the Comfortable Oracle: accepting answers because further questions are inconvenient. Fluency is what makes the questions feel inconvenient, because a smooth answer seems to close the subject while a rough one leaves an obvious place to push, so the smooth answer, which could most use a follow-up, is the one least likely to get it.

In the Practices loop, this Article belongs to Query and Witness. The practice is to ask in a form that can be checked and then to do the checking, preferring answers that come with their reasons and their sources and say how sure they are. An answer with no uncertainty in it at all deserves more suspicion than one with some, because the world rarely offers that much certainty, and when it does, it can usually show its work.
