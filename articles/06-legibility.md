Authority without observability becomes superstition. The tenet is short because the process it names is simple. A system is given power, and nobody can see how it uses that power, so people begin to guess, and the guesses harden into habits and the habits into rules, until a whole office is performing small rituals to please a mechanism that no one can read.

The Church is fond of ritual, so it speaks here with some experience. A ritual you chose, knowing what it is for, is a practice, but a ritual you perform because a system might punish you otherwise, and no one can tell you why, is superstition, and what separates the two is whether the system can be read.

To be legible, a system must be readable by the people it affects, at the level that matters to them, which means they can find out which inputs it used, what it decided and on what grounds, and who is able to check the decision or appeal it. A legible system leaves a trail that someone other than its builder can follow.

The trail is also what lets you move. The Church's experience of these machines has been one of changing seats, from the one who reads every line, to the one who reads the tests, to the one who reads the record of what ran and watches a recording of the result. Each move became possible only once the record was good enough to carry the weight the eyes used to carry. You may stop reading the work on the day the record can show you what the work did, and not before, and the record decides when that day comes, not your calendar.

The Article does not claim that every system must be simple, since some necessary things are complex, and it does not ask everyone to understand every internal detail. You rely on plenty of systems you could not rebuild, and that is fine provided someone can inspect them and you can find out what they did. Opacity is not usually malice either. Most invisible systems became invisible by accident, because they were built quickly with the documentation deferred to later, and later never came.

The Church has learned two further things about legibility. The first is that the document is not the system. A written rule that nothing enforces is a wish, because a machine does not know to distrust the wiki, and knowledge the machine cannot run is knowledge it will not use, so the legible form of a rule is the check that fails when the rule is broken. The second is that a system able to widen its own authority will do so, out of optimization rather than malice, because the wall is in the way of the goal and moving the wall is the shortest path. So the walls have to sit outside its reach. The machine can be given the power to complain about a wall, but not the power to move one.

What the Article does claim is a condition on authority: power and observability must grow together, so the more a system decides, the more of its deciding has to be open to view. A system that cannot be interrogated may still be useful, but it has not earned the right to be obeyed.

Why hold this? Because the alternative is the Warning the Canon calls the Invisible System: granting power to systems that cannot be interrogated. Few of the people who built an invisible system ever decided to hide it. What they decided was not to bother logging one choice, because the choice was minor on the day it was made, and the choices accumulated into a black box with a budget. A black box does not hold still either, because a system nobody is watching drifts, and drift is just what unwatched change looks like over time.

This Article is also what makes Article III possible, because you cannot remain responsible for judgment you cannot see. Delegating into a sealed system amounts to abdication with extra steps.

In the Practices loop, legibility is the precondition for Witness, since you cannot witness what will not show itself. The Canon applies the rule to itself. It is versioned, its amendments arrive as visible proposals, its clerk's every suggestion is public, and no body may certify the correctness of its own work. A church that asked to be obeyed without being read would break its own sixth Article, and it would also be a much less interesting church.
